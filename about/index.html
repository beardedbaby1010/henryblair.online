About me

Medical student, SUNY Downstate College of Medicine, Class of 2028 · Brooklyn, NY

I am interested in the gap between what medicine says should happen and what actually happens. A guideline can recommend something, a clinical trial can establish that it works, and a professional society can make it standard of care. But none of that means it is what is being done in practice. A lot of my research focuses on that gap.

My current work focuses on urology, and my favorite project is on treatment patterns and guideline concordance within prostate cancer care. I mainly use large-scale real-world databases to investigate questions about when patients receive treatment, who receives it, and where opportunities for better care are being missed. I am especially interested in building research projects from the ground up, including data extraction, question logic, cleaning data, and performing statistical analysis in a standardized, reproducible fashion. I generally prefer knowing exactly where a number came from rather than trusting it blindly just because it appeared in a spreadsheet.

I attended Emory University for my undergraduate education, where I studied neuroscience and spent four years researching epilepsy and autism. My honors thesis examined the relationship between anterior insula connectivity, the salience and ventral attention networks, and symptom severity. Before that, I worked mostly in wet labs studying epilepsy. Between the two experiences, I developed an appreciation for both experimental rigor and the occasional frustration of trying to make biological systems behave predictably.

I have always enjoyed the technical side of research. I write my own code and have for years. Today, I enjoy fine-tuning my own data extraction and analysis code, building automated workflows, and spending, admittedly, an unreasonable amount of time on Python, Zsh, Excel formulas, and SPSS replication. Most of my projects on this site are presented as interactive research hubs rather than static PDFs because I want people to be able to follow the methodology, inspect underlying logic, and understand how we got from point A to Z — that is, understand everything from the original population to the final result. If someone disagrees with my analysis, I’d much rather they find the exact place where I went wrong than have to take my word for it.

Outside of research, I have spent a fair amount of time teaching, mentoring, and advocating for students. I was an organic chemistry teaching assistant for two years, founded an education-equity nonprofit at Emory, and led the university’s autism advocacy organization. I have mentored incoming medical students, interviewed applicants, volunteered at free medical clinics, and served on the executive boards of various clubs in medical school.

I came to medicine through neuroscience, but my interest in urology developed during medical school. I remember calling my father, a urologist himself who had wanted me to become one too, partway through physiology to tell him that I finally understood why he liked it. In fairness, the deck was stacked: a urologist for a father, four brothers, and a urinal in the house I grew up in. I loved learning about the kidneys and doing our hemodynamics practice problems. I always found the kidneys to be particularly impressive. However, the nephrologists of the world unfortunately missed out on my genius, as during my third year of medical school, my love for the OR trumped any preceding possibility of my going into a non-surgical field.

I am currently completing a research fellowship at Maimonides Medical Center, working for their Department of Urology before returning for my final year of medical school at SUNY Downstate. My research spans dozens of projects within urologic oncology, urogynecology, functional urology, and much more.

Outside medicine, I play three instruments but am best at jazz trombone, I love to ski (more than anything in this world), go hiking or backpacking, swim, play pickup lacrosse or pickleball, spend time doing sudoku, and go on runs. Ultimately, my common theme is that I love figuring things out, building things, and working with people who are better at something than I am. I also like teaching, particularly when I can help someone understand something that initially seemed impossible. I think the best work, whether in medicine, research, or just about anything else, comes from being genuinely interested in the problem rather than simply wanting to have solved it. If medicine didn’t work out, I would most likely make a sharp left and go into finance. Nah, just kidding, I would never! I would either become a professional backgammon player or a professional free-ride ski instructor — ideally somewhere with good snow. I guess life has other plans for me though…
