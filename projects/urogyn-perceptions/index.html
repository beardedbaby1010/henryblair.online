How urology residents see urogynecology

Gina DeMeo · Henry M. Blair · Nina Robins · Luke Jenusaitis · Jubin Matloubieh

Exposure and mentorship are the standard explanation for why residents do or do not choose a subspecialty. We asked 115 urology residents across the country, and neither one explained who was interested. What did was gender, structured teaching, what residents believed the operating was like — and, above all, whether they had already made up their minds.

Faculty buy curriculum, not just contact. Programs with a URPS-trained urologist were far likelier to run formal didactics — 73.0% (65/89) versus 43.5% (10/23), p = 0.007. That matters, because didactics is the only kind of exposure that tracked with interest.

Fewer than a third had a mentor, and three quarters of those who did not believed one was available for the asking. Mentorship was also independent of exposure: it predicted neither whether meaningful exposure happened (12.1% vs 19.5% reporting none; OR 1.76, 95% CI 0.54–5.72; p = 0.34) nor when it happened (81.8% vs 69.5% by PGY-3; p = 0.18; median PGY-2 in both groups).

The cheapest finding in the study: programs could pair residents with URPS faculty rather than waiting to be asked.

The identity question the field argues about internally is, for residents, settled: 90.4% say URPS belongs in urology and the same proportion reject the idea that it is really a gynaecology subspecialty. The soft spot is further down the list — only 38.3% expect the career itself to satisfy them, and a third actively disagree.

Interest falls across training

Interest ran from 60.0% in PGY-1 to 27.3% in PGY-5, with none of the five PGY-6 residents interested (Cochran–Armitage z = −2.72, p = 0.007). Residents in PGY-4 or later reported interest less often than PGY-1 to 3 (31.8% vs 54.9%; OR 0.38; p = 0.016). Cross-sectionally this could be attrition or a cohort difference — either way the window is early.

The gender gap is not an exposure gap

Men and women reported similar didactic exposure (69.8% vs 62.5%; p = 0.42), similar clinical exposure (73.0% vs 79.2%; p = 0.45) and similar mentorship (25.4% vs 35.4%; p = 0.25). But didactics worked differently for each: among women, interest was 80.0% with didactics versus 38.9% without (OR 6.29, 1.71–23.14; p = 0.004); among men, 34.1% versus 26.3% (OR 1.45; p = 0.54). The gap was widest precisely where teaching was present.

Adjusted, mid-residency deciders (OR 23.26) and the undecided (OR 57.45) were far likelier than early deciders to report interest, both p < 0.001. Residents who named career uncertainty as a barrier were more interested, not less — 79.3% versus 34.9%, adjusted OR 5.92, p = 0.035.

Read carefully, those odds ratios are partly tautological: residents who have decided have mostly decided against. The honest version is descriptive — a third of residents are still undecided, and more than four in five of them are at least slightly interested. That is the group worth reaching.

What this cannot tell you

  • Cross-sectional, so the fall in interest across training years could be attrition or a cohort difference, and nothing here establishes cause.
  • Distribution through program leadership means there is no exact response rate, and residents interested in URPS were likelier to answer — so 46.1% is probably an overestimate.
  • Women were over-represented (41.7% of respondents versus roughly a third of US urology residents), and women reported more interest, which pushes the same way.
  • Residents cluster within programs, and program identity was not collected, so that clustering could not be modelled.
  • The multivariable models carry few events per covariate, which is why some intervals are very wide — the complexity-appeal odds ratios especially.
  • Faculty and fellowship presence, exposure and interest were all self-reported; three residents were unsure about their own program.
  • Free text was coded by one investigator without an inter-rater measure, and classifying a barrier as modifiable or fixed is a judgment others might make differently.

The question

Urogynecology and reconstructive pelvic surgery takes fellows from both urology and gynaecology, but few come from urology. Is that because residents never see the field, because they think it belongs to someone else, or because of something else entirely?

How it is being done

  • A 24-item REDCap survey emailed to program directors and coordinators at every ACGME-accredited US urology residency, July to August 2026, for forwarding to residents. Voluntary and anonymous; 116 returned, one blank, leaving 115.
  • It asked about training year, age and gender; program type; whether the program had fellowship-trained URPS faculty and an institutional fellowship; seven separate kinds of clinical exposure; the year of first meaningful exposure; mentorship and its influence.
  • Six perception statements on five-point Likert scales, eight predefined barriers, a free-text question on what would raise interest, and when the fellowship decision was made.
  • Proportions with Wilson score intervals; two-proportion z-tests with Woolf odds ratios; Mann–Whitney for timing; Cochran–Armitage for the trend across training years; Benjamini–Hochberg correction across the seven exposure types; then multivariable logistic regression, ordinal and dichotomised.
