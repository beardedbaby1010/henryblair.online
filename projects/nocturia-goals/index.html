What do patients with nocturia actually want treated?

Oliver B. Menken · Benjamin H. Parker · Henry M. Blair · Isabel X. Guan · Jeffrey P. Weiss, MD, PhD

Nocturia trials are built on a countable outcome: voids per night. It is easy to measure and easy to compare, and it may not be the thing patients are trying to change. A treatment that removes one void from three may or may not be a treatment that changes a life. So we asked.

The question

Guidelines and trials characterise nocturia treatment success almost entirely through two numbers: the change in mean nocturnal voids per night, and the proportion of patients reaching a 50% reduction. No minimal clinically important difference has ever been established empirically — in its absence, Cochrane reviewers adopted one fewer void per night as a working threshold.

But bother does not rise linearly with void count. Patients with one nightly episode can be meaningfully bothered, and the steepest rise in bother falls across the second and third. A drop from four voids to three may be nothing like a drop from two to one. And the count says nothing at all about which part of the symptom — the waking, the sleep, the urgency, the leakage — a patient most wants addressed.

How many nocturnal voids will patients tolerate before they want treatment, how much reduction has to happen before they would call a medication effective, and what do they want treated in the first place?

How it was done

The Epidemiology of Nocturnal Polyuria study (EpiNP) is a US population-based cross-sectional survey of community-dwelling adults aged 30 and over, recruited to approximate the general population by age, sex and race. This is a secondary analysis of its baseline web survey — specifically the nocturia treatment-preference and medication items — and does not use the three-day bladder diary component.

What was asked

  • Desire for treatment. Whether the respondent was bothered by nighttime urination enough to want to reduce it. Yes or no.
  • Tolerance threshold. How many nighttime urinations they were willing to live with without pursuing treatment.
  • Primary treatment goal. One of five mutually exclusive options: fewer nighttime urinations, better sleep, less nighttime urgency, no nighttime leakage, or other.
  • Meaningful-improvement threshold. How many fewer voids a night it would take to recognise that a medication was working. Secondary endpoint.

Cohort and analysis

Of the 10,190 EpiNP baseline respondents, 7,539 had complete data on every core preference variable and formed the primary complete-case cohort; records routed past the preference items by skip logic, or carrying placeholder or out-of-range values, were excluded before analysis. Counts are reported as median [IQR] and proportions with Wilson score intervals. Goal distributions were compared with Pearson chi-square tests, and two logistic models were fitted — one for wanting treatment, one for choosing a goal other than fewer voids. Analyses were run and independently validated in SPSS.

Most people were not asking for help. Only one in seven was bothered enough to want treatment at all, and the median respondent was prepared to live with one void a night. That is the backdrop against which a one-void reduction has to be judged.

Sleep alone — 29.0% — is chosen nearly twice as often as urgency and leakage combined. An endpoint that counts voids records none of it.

What counts as better

Among the 1,082 respondents who answered the secondary item, the median reduction needed to recognise that a medication was working was one fewer void per night [IQR 0–2]. That is the one number in this study that agrees with current practice: Cochrane’s working threshold of one void, adopted for want of an empirical anchor, turns out to be roughly where patients put it too.

So the magnitude trials use is about right. What they measure is not.

The two models point the same way. Overactive bladder and insomnia — the phenotypes most likely to want treatment at all — are also the ones most likely to want something other than a lower count. The patients a trial is most likely to enrol are the patients its endpoint serves worst.

What this cannot tell you

  • Cross-sectional and self-reported throughout, including every comorbidity: these are conditions respondents say they were diagnosed with, not chart diagnoses.
  • Respondents came from an online research panel, so internet access and panel membership are built into the sample even though recruitment targeted the general population by age, sex and race.
  • A single most-important goal was forced from five fixed options. Someone who wants both fewer voids and better sleep had to discard one.
  • The complete-case restriction drops 2,651 of 10,190 respondents. Those routed past the preference items by skip logic are not missing at random.
  • The subgroup comparisons are exploratory and were not corrected for multiple testing.
  • Preferences stated on a survey are not the same as choices made in a clinic with a prescription in front of you.

What comes next

If what patients want treated is not what trials measure, the argument follows for a goal-attainment endpoint — one that asks whether the outcome a patient named actually improved — reported alongside void count rather than instead of it.
