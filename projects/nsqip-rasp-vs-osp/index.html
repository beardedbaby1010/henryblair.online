The rapid rise of robot-assisted simple prostatectomy

Edward Wu · Henry M. Blair · Marino Kokolis-Lattanzio · Ariel Schulman

Robot-assisted simple prostatectomy (RASP) is increasingly used for large-gland benign prostatic hyperplasia, but national data on its uptake and early safety compared with open simple prostatectomy (OSP) are limited. We described national adoption of RASP and compared 30-day outcomes between approaches, overall and across patient subgroups.

The question

Robotic simple prostatectomy has all but replaced the open operation. What did that change in transfusion and complications?

How it is being done

  • We identified simple prostatectomies in 2022-2024 within the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (ACS NSQIP) - either RASP (robot used) or OSP (open, no robot).
  • Emergent cases, preoperative admission, moribund status, preoperative sepsis or ventilator dependence, and missing length of stay were excluded.
  • Robotic share was tested for linear trend.
  • Outcomes were compared with unadjusted relative risks and two-proportion or Fisher’s exact tests.
  • Subgroups were defined by anesthesia risk class, frailty, age ≥70 years, obesity and anemia.

What it shows so far

  • Of 3,033 simple prostatectomies, 2,580 (85.1%) were RASP, rising from 73.2% in 2022 to 91.2% in 2024 (p-trend<0.001).
  • RASP had lower transfusion within 72 hours (1.8% vs 18.3%; relative risk 0.10, 95% confidence interval 0.07-0.14), any complication (11.2% vs 28.0%), readmission (5.5% vs 8.2%) and reoperation (1.6% vs 4.2%).
  • Superficial wound infection (0.9% vs 2.6%) and kidney injury (0.7% vs 2.6%) were also less frequent; urinary infection, thromboembolism, pneumonia and sepsis did not differ.
  • Lower transfusion held in every subgroup.
RASP now accounts for more than 90% of simple prostatectomies in NSQIP hospitals and is associated with a tenfold lower transfusion rate and less than half the complication rate of OSP, a benefit that persists in older, frail and anemic patients. Comparisons are unadjusted and OSP patients carried more comorbidity, so selection bias is likely.
